01 WHAT?

Two Ideas, One Calendar

What if we could simplify a calendar that forces us to constantly check how dates and weekdays align?

The Equilune Calendar is built around a simple structure.

A week still has seven days.

Four weeks make 28 days. Our basic weekly rhythm remains unchanged.

At the end of each month, we add two “weekless days” — special days outside the weekly cycle.

These are called Neutral Days (N-days).

28 days + 2 Neutral Days = 30 days per month.

Neutral Days are intended to serve as regular days off, replacing the public holidays scattered throughout the current calendar.

If Saturdays and Sundays are also days off, every month would end with a four-day break.

A Special Year-End

December works a little differently.

After its regular 30 days, five additional Weekless Days are added, numbered December 31–35.

These are called Year-Crossing Days (X), distinct from the monthly Neutral Days.

Under this system, December 30 becomes New Year's Eve, December 31 becomes New Year's Day, and the New Year holiday period continues through December 35.

Imagine the final days of the year:

  • December 27–28: Saturday and Sunday
  • December 29–30: Neutral Days (N)
  • December 31–35: Year-Crossing Days (X)

If all these days are observed as days off, they create nine consecutive days of rest.

On January 1, the regular weekly cycle begins again.

In leap years, one additional Year-Crossing Day is added: December 36.

Predictable Time Off

In the Equilune Calendar, both Neutral Days and Year-Crossing Days are designed to be days off.

If Saturdays, Sundays, N days, and X days are all observed as holidays, the calendar provides 125 days off every year (126 in leap years).

Of course, not every profession follows weekends or public holidays.

For those working in essential services, shift-based industries, and other continuous operations, Equilune also explores alternative work structures designed to create more predictable and meaningful time off.

You can find these proposals in 05 THE MODULES.

And here is one of the simplest changes of all:

Every month has exactly the same arrangement of dates and weekdays.

The first day of one month is a Monday. The first day of the next month is also a Monday.

The calendar never needs to be rearranged from month to month.

What Happens to Holidays?

This brings us to Equilune's second central idea:

What if we separated public holidays from the occasions they commemorate?

Instead of abolishing holidays, Equilune unties time off from commemoration, offering a new way to preserve the meaning behind them.

Commemorative days, religious observances, and national celebrations would no longer automatically determine when people take time off.

Instead, Neutral Days and Year-Crossing Days would provide a regular, predictable structure for public holidays.

We could plan our breaks around the end of each month and enjoy an extended holiday at the turn of the year.

But what about the memories, prayers, traditions, and values carried by our existing holidays?

Equilune proposes bringing them together through Theme Weeks.

Twelve Months, Twelve Themes

The third week of each month could be dedicated to a particular theme.

That gives us twelve Theme Weeks each year.

Each country, community, or religious tradition could choose themes that reflect what matters most to them.

During a Theme Week, schools might invite students to explore selected topics through research projects.

Families might share stories and conversations.

Media organizations might highlight historical events, cultural traditions, or social questions connected to the theme.

None of this needs to be compulsory.

The purpose is simply to create a shared opportunity to remember, reflect, celebrate, or pray — each in our own way.

For example, in the United States, possible themes might include:

  • Independence and the Founding of the Nation Week
  • War and Veterans Remembrance Week
  • Great Figures in American History Week

In France, possible themes might include:

  • Saints and Religious Heritage Week
  • Liberty and Revolution Week
  • Labor and Workers Week

These are only illustrations. The themes themselves would belong to the communities that choose them.

Making Room for the Future

History does not end here.

New events will continue to shape our societies, our cultures, and our collective memories.

When something happens that deserves to be remembered for generations, what should we do?

Add another public holiday?

Remove an existing commemoration to make room for a new one?

Or leave these decisions entirely to those who happen to hold power at the time?

By separating commemorations from public holidays, Theme Weeks offer a more flexible way to recognize what matters to different communities.

They allow traditions to be preserved, new memories to be welcomed, and identities to evolve without constantly competing for a limited number of days off.
